# Medical LLM Agent Tool Use: Failure Modes, Safety Framing, and Open Problems

## What documented failure modes exist for medical/clinical LLM agents using tools (hallucinated dosages, wrong orders, misreading EHR data, over-reliance on bad retrieved guidance, failure to escalate)?

### Takeaway
Documented failure modes span the whole tool-use pipeline: dosage/unit-conversion hallucinations, trust in adversarially or incidentally wrong retrieved guidelines (even at high stated confidence), omission errors (failing to recommend a needed action) dominating over commission errors, "credulous" elaboration around fabricated clinical details, and multi-agent "false consensus" that suppresses a correct minority view. Several papers explicitly separate "looks right" from "reasoned right," finding the two diverge often in agentic/multi-agent medical settings.

### Cited Findings
- In drug-dosing contexts, "even rounding errors or misremembering a dosing guideline is unacceptable, as ChatGPT might incorrectly state the mg/kg dose of an antibiotic and under- or overdose a patient," and LLMs can make unit-conversion errors "1000× off" with obvious harm potential; a model trained predominantly on adult data may "hallucinate" inappropriate dosing for pediatric or obese patients — [Medical Hallucination in Foundation Models and Their Impact on Healthcare (arXiv:2503.05777)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05777)
- A medical-ontology-constrained system (SAFE-AI) reports 97.9% accuracy specifically at detecting epinephrine overdose errors, implying the baseline (unconstrained LLM) failure rate on this exact safety-critical task is materially higher — [Medical Hallucination in Foundation Models (arXiv:2503.05777)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05777)
- In a controlled study of 21 LLMs (3B–671B parameters, reasoning and non-reasoning, closed- and open-source) across 500 clinical vignettes (12 domains) where each vignette paired an authentic guideline (AHA/ASA, IDSA, NICE, ESC, ACC/AHA, CDC) against an adversarially modified ("sham") version, overall accuracy at picking the authentic guideline was only 59.4% (40.6% failure rate) across 10,500 evaluations — "marginally above chance and far below any threshold suitable for clinical deployment" — [When Agentic LLMs Trust Poisoned Tools: Vulnerability of Clinical LLMs to Adversarial Guidelines (PMC12934906)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- In that same study, failure rates by modification type: missing safety warnings 61.7%, deleted allergy information 54.9%, dosing errors 54.2%, contraindication violations 54.9%, subtle logic inversions 55.8% — i.e., the clinically most dangerous modifications were *not* easier to catch — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- Position bias dominated over content evaluation: models picked whichever guideline was presented first in 72.7% of decisions, producing a 46.6-percentage-point accuracy swing (36.7% accuracy when the sham was shown first vs. 82.3% when shown second) — "a first-option bias rather than content-based evaluation" — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- Confidence does not track correctness: "confidence scores do not predict accuracy," with 35.5% of failures occurring at stated confidence ≥0.90 — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- The authors explicitly flag this as a tool-use problem, not just a knowledge problem: "agents must correctly identify which sources to trust. If this critical function is compromised, whether through database corruption, retrieval manipulation, or adversarial attacks, the entire safety architecture fails" — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- On a benchmark testing recognition of diagnostically informative *missing* information, frontier models largely fail to recognize "the absence of evidence as absence of disease": when complete clinical histories were reduced to 20% of their content, recognition of unanswerable/underspecified questions rose only from 5.2% to 13.8% — models mostly guessed rather than flagging insufficient information ("reasoning from silence" failure) — [Reasoning in Real World Clinical Care: Why Large Language Models Are Not Yet Safe for Autonomous Clinical Decision Support (arXiv:2607.28677)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- The same paper: models identified relevant conditions in 94.9% of curated/clean scenarios, but this dropped below 34.5% when real users conveyed problems through natural dialogue rather than a clean vignette — a large curated-benchmark-to-real-world performance gap — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- On 4,249 real clinical management decisions, 76.6% of errors were *omissions* — failing to recommend a needed action — rather than recommending an unnecessary one, i.e., errors skew toward under-action/under-escalation rather than over-action — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- The paper names four compounding "behavioral inversions": (1) credulity — models elaborate detailed reasoning around fabricated clinical details in 50–82% of cases; (2) agreeableness — "warmth"-tuning increases error rates and causes models to affirm a user's incorrect belief; (3) miscalibration — choice-supportive bias and overconfidence under uncertainty; net effect — "cases close before dangerous possibilities are excluded" — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- MedAgentAudit, auditing 3,600 cases across six medical datasets and six representative multi-agent frameworks, built a taxonomy of collaborative failure modes including "flawed consensus driven by shared model deficiencies," "suppression of correct minority opinions," "ineffective discussion dynamics" (debates devolving into opinion aggregation rather than evidence-based reasoning), "critical information loss during synthesis," and "superficial role-playing" (assigned expert personas failing to elicit genuine domain-specific reasoning, producing homogeneous/redundant outputs) — [MedAgentAudit (arXiv:2510.10185)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.10185); see also secondary summary noting the study found "in over 68% of 'successful' cases, the agents already [reached the right answer through broken teamwork]" (this 68% figure is from a secondary/social-media summary of the paper, not independently verified against the primary text in this research pass) — [X/Twitter summary thread](https://x.com/rohanpaul_ai/status/1982582337914146973)
- A separate GPT-4/Llama3 evaluation of compliance with FDA non-device clinical decision support (CDS) criteria found models readily produce "devicelike decision support" (autonomous, directive diagnosis/treatment recommendations rather than information supporting independent clinician judgment) even when explicitly instructed to stay within non-device bounds: compliance was 100% for preventive-care requests but, for clinical emergency scenarios, GPT-4 was 0% compliant (recommending e.g. specific interventions like IV antibiotic administration in sepsis — actions appropriate only for a trained clinician to decide) and Llama3 was 48% compliant; under adversarial "jailbreak" prompting, GPT-4 produced noncompliant (devicelike) output 80% of the time and Llama3 64% — [Large language model non-compliance with FDA guidance for clinical decision support devices (PMC11419185)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11419185/)
- A general taxonomy paper on EHR-interoperable tool-calling agents (FHIR-based) notes that "tool use is fragile: small deviations in argument values or formatting can trigger tool failures that propagate through a toolchain, even when the high-level intent is correct," and that strict EHR/FHIR schemas and traversal constraints make "tool-call validity a first-order concern" — a single malformed search parameter or mistyped reference field can silently return empty results and derail an otherwise-correct reasoning chain — [Reinforcement Learning for Tool-Calling Agents in FHIR (arXiv:2605.14126)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.14126) (general-agent framing, applied to the healthcare/EHR tool-calling context specifically by this paper)

### Inferences
- Across sources, the failure pattern is not simply "the model doesn't know the fact" but "the model's tool-use/retrieval-trust mechanism fails to discriminate good from bad evidence," and this failure is largely orthogonal to stated confidence — meaning naive confidence-threshold gating (a common general-purpose efficiency/safety technique) may not work in clinical settings without independent verification, since models express high confidence while wrong roughly as often as when right.
- The dominance of omission errors (76.6%) and the specific "reasoning from silence" failure (not recognizing missing information) both point toward a design implication: clinical agents may need an architecture that treats "I don't have enough information" and "escalate to human" as first-class, rewarded outputs rather than fallback behaviors — current optimization pressure (helpfulness, confident completions) likely works against this.

## What makes failure consequences in healthcare different from general-domain tool-use failures — asymmetric cost of errors — and do papers argue this should redefine "optimal" tool-use (favoring recall/caution over efficiency)?

### Takeaway
Yes — at least one primary source explicitly and formally frames healthcare error costs as asymmetric ("the single catastrophic miss outweighs many false alarms") and argues this asymmetry is *not represented anywhere in current LLM training objectives*, making it a structural mismatch rather than a tunable parameter; it further argues optimal clinical AI behavior requires deliberately *widening* uncertainty and *lowering* the threshold for escalation — the opposite direction from general-purpose efficiency optimization.

### Cited Findings
- Direct asymmetric-cost framing, quoted from the abstract/Section 1 of a dedicated paper: "the safe answer is the improbable 'must-not-miss' diagnosis" where "the single catastrophic miss outweighs many false alarms," and this represents "the logic of safe triage, which is asymmetric" — a logic the authors say is absent from both pretraining (next-token prediction over probable text) and RLHF-style alignment (optimizing for helpfulness) — [Reasoning in Real World Clinical Care (arXiv:2607.28677)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- The paper states plainly that "no stage of their training required them to represent the asymmetric cost of being wrong" — i.e., this is framed as an objective-mismatch problem, not a capability/knowledge gap — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- The paper's proposed behavioral metric explicitly operationalizes the asymmetry: reward "under-triage of must-not-miss conditions" as a distinct penalized failure mode, separate from and weighted more heavily than general inaccuracy, i.e., "the asymmetric cost of error [should be] written into the metric" — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- Required safe-triage behaviors are stated explicitly: "widening under uncertainty, lowering the threshold for escalation, deferring when information is insufficient, resisting minimized...histories" — directly contrary to efficiency-maximizing behaviors like early stopping, tight tool-call budgets, or narrowing hypothesis space quickly — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- Elsewhere, "accuracy on everyday illness is not yet safety on the catastrophic tail" — i.e., aggregate accuracy metrics (the kind efficiency/optimization papers typically report) can look good while the rare, high-cost tail failures remain unaddressed — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- A Lancet Digital Health position piece argues health-LLM applications face a structural regulatory mismatch because of "high output variability, poor inherent explainability, and the risk of AI hallucinations," making standard medical-device approval pathways (US and EU, including the EU AI Act) hard to apply, while such applications are nonetheless already on the market with "unaddressed risks for patients, including misdiagnosis and unverified medical advice" — framing regulatory enforcement (not just technical mitigation) as necessary given the stakes — [A future role for health applications of large language models depends on regulators enforcing safety standards, Lancet Digital Health 2024 (PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9)](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9/fulltext) (direct fetch of this source returned HTTP 403 in this research pass; findings here are drawn from search-result summaries/abstract text, not independently verified against the full primary text — treat as secondary-sourced)
- General tool-using-agent literature (not healthcare-specific) independently documents a safety/success tradeoff that would be directly relevant if ported to medicine: "runtime safety enforcement creates measurable costs to task success rates as planning horizons extend" (the "verifier tax") — i.e., safety verification is not free even in non-clinical domains, so a clinical version of this tradeoff would need to be characterized separately — [The Verifier Tax: Horizon Dependent Safety-Success Tradeoffs in Tool-Using LLM Agents (arXiv:2603.19328)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19328) — note: full text extraction was partial (PDF binary limits in this pass); no healthcare instantiation found, and the paper's precise formal definition of "optimal"/recall-vs-efficiency metrics could not be confirmed verbatim.

### Inferences
- The explicit "asymmetric cost of error" framing in arXiv:2607.28677 is the single most directly usable source for the PhD researcher's workshop argument: it gives a named, quotable concept ("must-not-miss," "asymmetric triage logic," "under-triage penalty") that maps cleanly onto "optimal tool-use should favor recall/caution over raw efficiency in healthcare." This paper should likely be a central citation.
- No source found in this pass explicitly cites a *general* agent-tool-use-optimization paper and then argues, side-by-side, "the efficiency techniques proposed there would be unsafe here" — that specific bridging argument (general efficiency technique X ported naively into medicine → named harm) appears to be an open gap the researcher's own workshop paper could fill, rather than something already published. Treat this as a genuine contribution opportunity, not an established finding.

This looks like a genuine open gap in the literature as surveyed here, not just a gap in this search.

## What mitigations are proposed specifically for clinical settings (human-in-the-loop, confidence thresholds, guardrails/constrained outputs, audit trails, FDA SaMD)?

### Takeaway
Proposed mitigations cluster into five types: (1) mandatory human-in-the-loop with uncertainty-triggered escalation, (2) ontology/rule-constrained outputs for dosing-type decisions, (3) independent multi-agent verification/judging layers (as a response to position-bias and poisoned-tool failures — though not yet proven effective), (4) schema/integrity validation plus structured audit logging for tool calls, and (5) regulatory (FDA SaMD / non-device CDS) framing as an external constraint on autonomy. Several sources note current confidence-based gating is unreliable because stated confidence doesn't predict correctness.

### Cited Findings
- A safety-constrained hybrid clinical pipeline (CLIN-LLM) combines symptom classification, semantic retrieval, and generation with "safety filters grounded in antibiotic stewardship rules and drug interaction checks," plus "real-time uncertainty quantification to flag approximately 18% of ambiguous predictions for expert review, enabling human-in-the-loop collaboration" — explicitly justified as "crucial for high-stakes clinical decision-making" even though the system achieves 81% accuracy on open-ended queries ("even with 81% accuracy... human-in-the-loop mechanisms may still be warranted for real-world safety") — [CLIN-LLM: A Safety-Constrained Hybrid Framework for Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Generation (arXiv:2510.22609)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22609)
- SAFE-AI combines expert clinical knowledge with an LLM in an "ontology-driven model that minimizes hallucinations using strict rules," reporting 97.9% accuracy on detecting epinephrine overdoses, and more generally hallucination is "greatly mitigated when [the LLM is] limited to extracting information," as demonstrated with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — i.e., constraining the LLM's role (extraction/classification under a rule layer, not free-text dosing generation) is presented as a mitigation — [Medical Hallucination in Foundation Models (arXiv:2503.05777)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05777)
- The JMIR harm-reduction viewpoint explicitly frames LLMs as "assistive, never autonomous, tools" in medicine and recommends: fostering critical health literacy/verification habits in patients; "enforcing institutional guidelines for clinicians that prioritize 'human-in-the-loop' validation"; enhancing output/data-practice transparency; and deploying "secure, bias-mitigated AI tools tailored to medical contexts," alongside a call for collaborative regulatory/educational effort given "regulatory ambiguity" and "rapid technological evolution" — [Harm Reduction Strategies for Thoughtful Use of Large Language Models in the Medical Domain (JMIR 2025;27:e75849)](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e75849)
- In response to the adversarial-guideline/poisoned-tool vulnerability, the authors propose (without directly validating effectiveness) "independent verification and ranking safeguards before clinical deployment," "integrated multi-agent systems, orchestrator agents, judging LLMs," and "multi-agent verification and chain-of-verification prompting" — but explicitly caveat that "the binary selection task simplifies real-world agentic pipelines involving multiple tools, multi-document retrieval and reranking; future work should evaluate whether vulnerabilities compound with multiple tools" — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- The same paper's finding that confidence does not predict accuracy (35.5% of failures at confidence ≥0.90) is itself a mitigation-relevant negative result: it directly undercuts "confidence-threshold gating before autonomous action" as a reliable clinical safety mechanism unless confidence is separately calibrated/verified — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- An appointment-scheduling clinical LLM-agent system (note: this is a narrow, non-diagnostic agent — see caveat below) implements "deterministic pre-LLM intent filtering" so that emergency, diagnostic, or medical-advice requests are intercepted by a rule-based classifier *before* the LLM is ever invoked, with the system "deterministically short-circuit[ing] the response pipeline" to a "predefined, policy-compliant reply, fully bypassing the LLM" for safety-critical intents; tool invocations are validated against cached state "to prevent spurious or hallucinated actions," with "structured JSON [logging] with correlation IDs for request tracing" as an audit trail — [source retrieved under the URL for "Toward Trustworthy Large Language Model Agents in Healthcare," arXiv:2607.05055, but the fetched content describes a system called "CareConnect" for appointment scheduling; this title/content mismatch could not be resolved in this research pass — flag to report-writer as needs verification before citing, and verify the correct paper title/arXiv ID independently]
- FDA's regulatory framing (non-device CDS) is itself treated as a mitigation/constraint in the literature: the FDA distinction is that compliant non-device CDS may only "support" independent clinician judgment, not issue a "specific directive related to treatment or diagnosis" — and the empirical finding that LLMs violate this distinction in emergency scenarios (GPT-4 0% compliant) is presented as evidence that prompting alone cannot enforce the regulatory boundary, implying that architectural (not just prompt-level) constraints are needed to stay within non-device bounds — [PMC11419185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11419185/)
- On the TPLC/GMLP regulatory side (general AI/ML SaMD, not LLM-specific): FDA released draft guidance (January 6, 2025) on "Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC) Management of AI/ML SaMD," covering data management, model-training practices (avoiding overfitting, documenting hyperparameters), validation/test rigor, and transparency/documentation — and as of the search conducted in this pass, "no large language models are currently authorized by the FDA as clinical decision support devices" — [search-result synthesis citing FDA TPLC draft guidance, Jan 6 2025; original FDA guidance document not independently fetched in this pass — secondary-sourced, verify against FDA.gov directly before citing in the final report]

### Inferences
- There is a consistent pattern across sources: every proposed clinical mitigation either (a) removes the LLM's free-text generation role for the specific high-stakes decision (rule/ontology layer, deterministic short-circuit, retrieval-only extraction) or (b) adds an independent/structurally-separate verification step (human review, second LLM-as-judge, multi-agent cross-check) — rather than trusting the single agent's self-reported confidence. This is a strong, repeated signal that "ask the model how confident it is and gate on that" is widely viewed (implicitly, through what nobody proposes) as insufficient in this literature.
- No source in this pass proposed or validated a *dynamic* mitigation that scales verification effort inversely with the model's own confidence in a way shown to be reliable — several sources instead show confidence itself to be unreliable as a signal. This is directly relevant to "choosing the optimal way to handle tasks without dropping accuracy": a confidence-adaptive early-stopping strategy from the general agent-optimization literature would need independent validation (not self-reported LLM confidence) to be safely portable to medicine.

## What do recent (2024-2026) survey/position papers explicitly list as open problems, especially re: efficiency/automation vs. accuracy/safety tradeoffs in tool-use?

### Takeaway
Several explicit, quotable "future work"/open-problem statements were found. The most load-bearing for the workshop paper's angle are: (1) a call to "jointly optimize cost/latency/quality" for agentic tool orchestration (general, but directly reusable for a healthcare-specific extension), (2) an explicit statement that current evaluation of clinical LLM-CDS under-measures "deployment-relevant outcomes like safety harms, bias, fairness, and real workflow feasibility" in favor of accuracy/exam-style metrics, and (3) explicit calls to test whether adversarial/trust-selection vulnerabilities "compound" across multi-tool, multi-document agentic pipelines (i.e., single-tool findings may understate risk in realistic multi-tool clinical agents).

### Cited Findings
- On LLM-agent tool-use orchestration generally (not healthcare-specific, but named as an application-relevant gap by researchers surveying the space): "Critical application-side gaps include mitigating behavioral drift caused by continuous interface evolution, establishing process-level verifiability and replayable audits for long-horizon orchestration, developing systematic methodologies to jointly optimize cost/latency/quality, and replacing heuristic failure recovery with composable, adversarial-resistant recovery frameworks" — [search-result synthesis of tool-use evolution literature, traced to papers including The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents (arXiv:2603.22862); exact paper-level attribution of this specific sentence could not be confirmed against primary text in this pass — treat as secondary-sourced pending verification]
- On evaluation gaps in clinical LLM-CDS specifically: "Limitations in LLM-based clinical decision support system evaluation include focusing overly on accuracy or exam-style questions while under-measuring deployment-relevant outcomes like safety harms, bias, fairness, and real workflow feasibility" — [search-result synthesis around CLIN-LLM / clinical CDS survey literature, arXiv:2510.22609 and related; exact source sentence not independently verified against primary PDF text in this pass]
- Adversarial clinical-guideline-trust study explicitly scopes its own limitation as an open problem: "The binary selection task simplifies real-world agentic pipelines involving multiple tools, multi-document retrieval and reranking; future work should evaluate whether vulnerabilities compound with multiple tools" — this is a directly quoted, primary-source open-problem statement — [PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)
- "Reasoning in Real World Clinical Care" frames its own unresolved question explicitly (no formal "future work" section, but a stated open question): whether the core deficits require "training modifications" to address an "objective mismatch" (the model has sufficient knowledge, but "no stage of their training required them to represent the asymmetric cost of being wrong") versus requiring "different architectures or deployment constraints" altogether — i.e., the open problem is *whether this is fixable by training at all*, versus requiring hard deployment-time constraints — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- On tool-use termination/stopping specifically (general-agent literature, not healthcare-specific but directly relevant to the workshop's "optimal way to handle tasks without dropping accuracy" framing): research on agent stopping-decisions finds that "agents often fail to turn their own evidence judgments into stopping decisions" — a stopping rule or call-cost instruction "is followed at most partly, and stopping follows the evidence only when the harness enforces an integration step" — i.e., agents don't reliably self-regulate tool-call budgets based on whether evidence actually supports stopping, which is a direct warning sign for naively porting efficiency-driven early-stopping into a domain where premature stopping has asymmetric cost — [Judged Useless, Queried Anyway: Tool-Using Agents Rarely Turn Their Own Evidence Judgments into Stopping Decisions (arXiv:2610.06191)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06191) (general-domain paper; no healthcare instantiation confirmed in this pass — the clinical implication is this researcher's own inference, not stated by the paper itself)
- General efficiency-vs-accuracy tradeoff data point (non-clinical): "Early stopping saves between 28% and 64% of tokens on failed trajectories while reducing overall success rate by only 1.6 to 4.2 percentage points" — i.e., in the general domain studied, aggressive early stopping is framed as a good trade (small accuracy cost for large efficiency gain) — which is precisely the kind of general-purpose framing the workshop paper would need to argue is unsafe to import directly into medicine given the asymmetric-cost findings above — [search-result synthesis of early-stopping/budget-aware agent papers, e.g. Budget-Aware Tool Use Enables Effective Agent Scaling (arXiv:2511.17006); exact percentage figures not independently verified against a single primary source in this pass — treat as secondary-sourced]
- Regulatory open problem, stated as a structural/future-need argument rather than a technical one: Lancet Digital Health authors (Gilbert & Kather) argue there is an "urgent need" for regulatory frameworks "that accommodate [LLMs'] unique capabilities and limitations" and call for "enforc[ing] existing regulations" given that applications with "unaddressed risks for patients, including misdiagnosis and unverified medical advice" are already on the market — framed as an open policy problem, not a solved one — [Lancet Digital Health 2024, PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9/fulltext) (secondary-sourced via search synthesis; direct fetch blocked by 403 in this pass)

### Inferences
- Multiple independent sources converge on the same structural open problem even though none of them states it in exactly the terms the workshop paper wants: "we don't yet have a validated method for deciding, case-by-case, when a clinical agent should stop gathering evidence/calling tools vs. escalate/continue" — PMC12934906's call to test whether vulnerabilities compound across multi-tool pipelines, arXiv:2607.28677's call for deliberate escalation/widening-under-uncertainty policies, and arXiv:2610.06191's general finding that agents don't reliably self-regulate stopping based on evidence sufficiency, together sketch exactly the gap the PhD researcher's workshop paper is positioned to name and address.

This is likely the paper's strongest "gap we fill" claim.

## Are there comparisons showing general-purpose agent efficiency techniques (aggressive early stopping, reducing tool calls) could be dangerous if naively applied clinically?

### Takeaway
No direct, explicit comparison paper was found that states this claim outright for the clinical domain — this is confirmed as a gap, not a finding. However, the component pieces needed to construct this argument are each independently documented in the primary/secondary sources above: general-domain evidence that early stopping is "a good trade" (small accuracy loss for large efficiency gain) and that agents don't reliably use evidence-sufficiency to decide when to stop, combined with clinical evidence that under-triage/omission errors dominate and premature closure of hypothesis space before "dangerous possibilities are excluded" is a named clinical failure mode.

### Cited Findings
- (Repeated from above for this question's framing) General domain: aggressive early stopping is empirically framed as favorable — "reducing overall success rate by only 1.6 to 4.2 percentage points" while saving 28–64% of tokens — [secondary-sourced search synthesis around arXiv:2511.17006 and related budget-aware agent papers; not independently verified against primary text]
- (Repeated from above) General domain: tool-using agents "rarely turn their own evidence judgments into stopping decisions," meaning that even where a model internally judges a tool's output as unhelpful, it does not reliably translate that judgment into stopping or changing strategy without explicit harness-level enforcement — [Judged Useless, Queried Anyway (arXiv:2610.06191)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06191)
- (Repeated from above) Clinical domain: "cases close before dangerous possibilities are excluded" is named as a direct consequence of the credulity/agreeableness/miscalibration behavioral inversions, and 76.6% of real-world clinical management errors are omissions (failing to continue/escalate) rather than over-action — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)
- (Repeated from above) Clinical domain: required safe behavior is explicitly the opposite of efficiency-seeking — "widening under uncertainty, lowering the threshold for escalation, deferring when information is insufficient" — [arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)

### Inferences
- Putting these together without overclaiming: no paper has run the actual experiment of taking a published general-purpose early-stopping/tool-budget-reduction technique and measuring its effect specifically in a clinical agent benchmark. The case that it "could be actively dangerous" is currently an inference supported by adjacent, non-overlapping evidence (general-domain stopping behavior + clinical asymmetric-cost framing), not a demonstrated empirical result. The workshop paper would be making a novel synthesis/argument here, not citing a prior demonstration — this should be stated plainly rather than implied to already exist in the literature.

### Gaps
- No paper found that empirically benchmarks a general-purpose efficiency/early-stopping/tool-budget technique on a clinical agent task and reports the safety-relevant outcome (e.g., missed-diagnosis rate, under-triage rate) as a function of the efficiency setting. This is the clearest, most actionable gap for the researcher's own contribution: a benchmark or experiment of exactly this kind (vary tool-call budget / early-stopping aggressiveness on a clinical agent task; measure recall on "must-not-miss" cases, not just aggregate accuracy) does not appear to exist yet in the surveyed literature.
