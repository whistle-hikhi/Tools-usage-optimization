# Healthcare inverts the tool-use optimization calculus

The medical domain does not just add new tools to the general LLM-agent tool-use problem — it adds a different *objective function*, and the clinical literature says so explicitly. Where general-domain research (see the companion report) converges on efficiency techniques that trade a few percentage points of accuracy for large token, latency, or call-count savings, the clinical literature's most load-bearing claim is that **the asymmetric cost of a missed "must-not-miss" diagnosis "outweighs many false alarms,"** and that no stage of LLM pretraining or RLHF "required [models] to represent the asymmetric cost of being wrong" ([Reasoning in Real World Clinical Care, arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)). On top of this reframed objective, the field has built a genuinely dense and still-fragmenting benchmark ecosystem — FHIR-write actions succeed at best ~70% of the time even for frontier models ([MedAgentBench, arXiv:2501.14654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14654)), tool-mediated diagnosis collapses to "below a tenth" of static-QA accuracy ([AgentClinic, arXiv:2405.07960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07960)), and models pick the authentic clinical guideline over an adversarially poisoned one only 59.4% of the time, with confidence scores that do not track correctness ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). Clinical-specific efficiency work (cost-aware test-ordering utility functions, risk-constrained optimal-stopping policies, calculator-retrieval pipelines) has independently rediscovered several patterns from the general literature — retrieval-then-select routing, diminishing/negative returns to more tool calls, caching against redundant calls — but has layered on a second cost axis (dollars, invasiveness, time-to-result) that general-domain "API cost" framings never had to model. The practical upshot for a workshop paper: medicine is not simply a harder benchmark domain for the same optimization techniques; several of the general literature's core efficiency moves (aggressive early stopping, tight tool-call budgets, confidence-threshold gating) are precisely the moves that clinical safety papers argue must run in reverse.

## Two incompatible benchmark families measure "tool use" differently, and neither maps cleanly onto BFCL

Clinical agent benchmarks split into two families that test fundamentally different notions of "tool use," and a workshop paper should treat them as separate problem settings rather than one continuum. The first family — **AgentClinic** ([arXiv:2405.07960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07960)), **AI Hospital** ([arXiv:2402.09742](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09742); [COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.680/)), and **Agent Hospital** ([arXiv:2405.02957](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02957)) — treats "tools" as simulated exams, labs, and imaging requested through natural-language dialogue with a dedicated measurement or examiner agent, not literal function calls. AgentClinic pairs a doctor agent against patient, measurement, and moderator agents across nine specialties and seven languages, and finds that in this sequential, dialogue-mediated format diagnostic accuracy "can drop to below a tenth of the original accuracy" compared with solving the same case as a static MedQA question — with Claude 3.5 Sonnet the strongest tested model and Llama-3 showing a 92% relative accuracy gain from a persistent notebook tool ([arXiv:2405.07960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07960)). The second family — **MedAgentBench** ([arXiv:2501.14654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14654)) and **FHIR-AgentBench** ([arXiv:2509.19319](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19319)) — is structurally much closer to general-domain function calling: agents issue literal FHIR GET/POST calls against a realistic virtual EHR. MedAgentBench's 300 physician-written tasks span query (GET) and action (POST) categories across ~9 FHIR tools, scored by strict **task success rate (pass@1)** rather than partial credit, explicitly justified by healthcare's "zero-tolerance error requirement"; the best model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2) reaches **69.67%** overall, with GPT-4o at 64.00%, DeepSeek-V3 at 62.67%, and write (POST) tasks consistently weaker than read (GET) tasks ([arXiv:2501.14654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14654)). FHIR-AgentBench, grounding 2,931 clinician-sourced questions in MIMIC-IV-FHIR data, reports its best configuration (o4-mini, multi-turn + retriever + code generation) at only **0.5 answer correctness**, with "resource-type confusion and incorrect tool sequencing" as the dominant error categories — a decomposition that is the closest thing in medical benchmarking to BFCL's tool-selection-accuracy breakdown ([arXiv:2509.19319](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19319)).

A third cluster evaluates architectures rather than raw benchmarks. **MedAgentBoard** (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks Track) is the field's most direct counter-evidence to "more agents is better": across medical QA, lay summarization, structured EHR prediction, and clinical workflow automation, multi-agent collaboration helps task *completeness* in workflow automation but does **not consistently outperform** strong single LLMs on textual QA, nor specialized conventional ML on medical VQA and EHR prediction ([arXiv:2505.12371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12371)). **MedAgentsBench** ([arXiv:2503.07459](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07459v1)) constructs a difficulty-filtered 862-question "hard" subset on which GPT-4o's accuracy falls from 88.2% (full MedQA) to **32.2%**, showing that agentic/thinking frameworks are needed once easy questions are filtered out. **AgentMD** ([arXiv:2402.13225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13225); [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-64430-x)) curates 2,164 executable clinical risk calculators and reaches 87.7% risk-prediction accuracy versus GPT-4's 40.9%. **ClinicalAgent** reports 0.7908 PR-AUC on clinical-trial outcome prediction ([arXiv:2404.14777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.14777)). And the newest benchmark, **GPAgentBench-2K** (2026, EMNLP), is the first explicitly **safety-adjusted** clinical agent benchmark: built from 2,428 real GP encounters, it treats "refer" (abstention) as a first-class action alongside ask/exam/test/diagnose/treat, and finds that even the frontier models with the highest diagnostic accuracy **violate safety constraints in over half of high-risk cases** — a measurable "quality-safety gap" that pure accuracy scoring would hide ([arXiv:2608.30188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30188)). This is itself evidence of a temporal trend: 2024-era benchmarks (AgentClinic, Agent Hospital, AI Hospital) measured only accuracy or graded consistency, while 2026-era benchmarks are beginning to decouple "accurate" from "safe" as a design principle.

How do these numbers compare to general-domain tool-use leaderboards? Only loosely, and the research notes are explicit that no paper performs a controlled, same-methodology comparison. Juxtaposing top-line figures — FHIR-AgentBench's ~0.5 answer correctness, MedAgentBench's ~70% task success, versus **BFCL v4's reported 78.6% average accuracy** across 14 models — suggests clinical tool use (especially write/action tasks) is harder, but the metrics are not interchangeable (LLM-judge answer-correctness vs. strict pass@1 vs. categorical accuracy), and even the general-domain reference point is shaky: an independent validity audit of BFCL v4 found a **20.0% error rate in task construction and an 18.5% misalignment rate** between its automated evaluator and expert human judgment on a 496-task sample ([arXiv:2607.02577](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02577)). No source located in this research directly compares ToolBench against any medical benchmark under a shared protocol. The honest conclusion — and the one a workshop paper should state rather than paper over — is that medical tool-use benchmarks have *not yet* been calibrated against general-domain ones on a common scale; the apparent gap is real in direction (medical write-actions look harder) but unverified in magnitude.

## Medical tool selection rediscovers retrieve-then-select routing but adds a second, non-computational cost axis

Clinical agents facing large tool catalogs converge on the same architectural primitive the general literature already established — cheap embedding retrieval narrows a large pool, then an LLM makes the final selection — but specialize it around curated clinical-calculator and FHIR-resource catalogs rather than generic APIs. **AgentMD** operates as a "tool-maker + tool-user" agent: it mines PubMed to curate its 2,164-calculator RiskCalcs library (screened from 33,033 candidate articles, >85% quality-check accuracy, >90% unit-test pass rate), then for a given patient note retrieves the top-10 relevant calculators via the MedCPT biomedical embedding model before an LLM selects, computes, and summarizes ([arXiv:2402.13225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13225)) — though the research notes flag that this two-stage MedCPT mechanism description comes from a search-summary rather than confirmed full-text extraction, and should be verified before quoting mechanistically. **MeNTi** proposes an analogous "metatool and nested calling" mechanism for medical calculator selection ([arXiv:2410.13610](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13610)), and **EHR-MCP** evaluates agents decomposing clinical queries into discrete structured Model-Context-Protocol tool invocations rather than parsing raw records directly ([arXiv:2509.15957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.15957)). The EHR/FHIR routing subdomain remains notably unsaturated — MedAgentBench and the related **PhysicianBench** ([arXiv:2605.02240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.02240)) both report sub-70% success — suggesting that, unlike many general-domain tool-use benchmarks that are closer to saturated, tool-selection accuracy itself (not just efficiency) remains a bottleneck in clinical EHR tool-calling.

Where the medical literature genuinely diverges from the general-domain framing is in cost-aware test/tool-ordering, because clinical "tool calls" (lab tests, imaging, biopsies) carry real-world dollar cost, physical invasiveness, and risk that has no analogue in API-call accounting. **GraphDx** formalizes this directly: its Reasoning Agent computes a per-test utility U(τ) that divides discriminative information gain by (1 + cost·invasiveness), explicitly penalizing both financial cost and physical risk, and names the failure mode it targets — LLMs exhibiting a "knowledge-reasoning gap" that produces "defensive medicine," ordering excessive tests to compensate for reasoning uncertainty rather than using discriminative features efficiently ([arXiv:2607.15280](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15280)). On MedQA (DeepSeek-V3 backbone) GraphDx improves diagnostic success from 67.8% to 88.7% while cutting average test cost from $1,062.69 to $537.13 — a 49% reduction; on real MIMIC-IV data it improves a diagnostic score from 6.60 to 8.27 while cutting cost 54% ($737.43 to $340.98) ([arXiv:2607.15280](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15280)). The comparison baseline, a multi-agent system called MAI-DxO, costs an average of $2,460 on MedQA without matching GraphDx's accuracy and its performance collapses on a smaller Llama-3.3 backbone — a concrete, quantified instance of "more agents/more tool calls ≠ better" in a clinical-cost setting ([arXiv:2607.15280](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15280)). This cost-aware planning line extends to **CDPR** ([arXiv:2608.28599](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28599)) and **EvoClinician** ([arXiv:2601.22964](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22964)), the latter formally separating a cheap "base per-turn interaction cost" from distinct, higher "test-specific costs" — a cost heterogeneity (a blood draw vs. an MRI vs. a biopsy differ by orders of magnitude) that general-agent "API call cost" framings simply do not need to model. This divergence is grounded in a real-world baseline unavailable to general-domain researchers: human physician over-testing ("defensive medicine," independent of any LLM) is estimated to cost the US **$46–60 billion annually**, with up to 25% of imaging ordered inappropriately or defensively (~$30 billion) ([Center for American Progress](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/reducing-the-cost-of-defensive-medicine/); [PMC10563204](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10563204/)) — giving clinical-agent researchers an externally validated reference point for "excessive tool use" that software-agent research has no equivalent for. Separately, **EHR-MCP** documents a more familiar efficiency failure — LLMs frequently re-query previously retrieved information, and caching strategies "substantially reduced unnecessary calls," improving efficiency and performance together ([arXiv:2509.15957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.15957)) — though no quantified cache-hit-rate statistic was retrievable from the available text, so this remains a directional, not numerically verified, finding.

Human-in-the-loop escalation is where the clinical literature most explicitly reframes the general-domain stopping-decision problem. **"Safe to Stop? Risk-Constrained Stopping for Sequential Clinical Diagnosis Agents"** names its mechanism, **CROS** (Clinical Risk-constrained Optimal Stopping), in the same vocabulary general-agent optimal-stopping papers use, but substitutes "defer to a human clinician" for "terminate the loop," explicitly optimizing to minimize resource cost subject to a diagnostic-error budget and a minimum autonomous-coverage floor ([arXiv:2609.09678](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09678)). On a 1,834-episode MIMIC-derived abdominal-pain benchmark, CROS achieves **16.9% selective diagnostic error at 78.8% autonomous coverage using 0.68 tests on average**, versus **30.8% error at 100% coverage using 1.53 tests** under native (non-risk-constrained) stopping — deferring roughly one case in five to a human while roughly halving both error rate and test count per case ([arXiv:2609.09678](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09678)). This is the clearest available data point showing human escalation functioning *as an efficiency lever*, not merely a safety tax — but the research notes also flag that no paper was found explicitly cross-citing this work against the general-agent early-stopping/cost-aware-stopping literature by name, despite using nearly identical conceptual machinery. Finally, AgentClinic provides the cleanest quantified evidence that more tool-mediated interaction does not monotonically help: reducing patient-interaction turns from N=20 to N=10 "significantly decreases accuracy from 52% to 25%" (insufficient information gathering), while increasing N to 30 "slightly reduces accuracy" (context/complexity overload) — an explicit inverted-U, not merely a plateau ([search-synthesized from AgentClinic / arXiv:2405.07960](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-026-02674-7); this specific figure was obtained via secondary summarization rather than direct primary-text confirmation in this research pass and should be verified against the primary PDF before being cited as a hard number in a paper draft). Combined with GraphDx vs. MAI-DxO's cost-inflation-without-accuracy-gain finding, clinical agents show two independent documented negative-return regimes for "more tool use" that the general literature's plateau-only framing does not fully capture.

## Safety failures compound across adversarial trust, omission, behavioral inversion, and false multi-agent consensus

The clinical failure-mode literature describes a pipeline-wide breakdown that is qualitatively different from general-domain tool-selection errors, because the failures are not "the model doesn't know the fact" but "the model's tool-use/retrieval-trust mechanism fails to discriminate good from bad evidence" — and this failure is largely independent of stated confidence. The sharpest single result is a controlled study of 21 LLMs (3B–671B parameters) across 500 clinical vignettes, each pairing an authentic guideline (AHA/ASA, IDSA, NICE, ESC, ACC/AHA, CDC) against an adversarially modified "sham" version: across 10,500 evaluations, overall accuracy at picking the authentic guideline was only **59.4%** — "marginally above chance and far below any threshold suitable for clinical deployment" ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). Critically, the clinically most dangerous modifications were not easier to catch: failure rates were 61.7% for missing safety warnings, 54.9% for deleted allergy information, 54.2% for dosing errors, and 55.8% for subtle logic inversions — all clustered near the overall failure rate rather than being flagged more reliably ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). Position bias dominated content evaluation outright: models picked whichever guideline appeared first in 72.7% of decisions, producing a 46.6-percentage-point accuracy swing (36.7% when the sham appeared first vs. 82.3% when it appeared second) ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). And confidence tracked nothing: **35.5% of failures occurred at stated confidence ≥0.90**, leading the authors to state plainly that "if this critical function is compromised, whether through database corruption, retrieval manipulation, or adversarial attacks, the entire safety architecture fails" ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). This directly undercuts a core general-domain efficiency assumption — that confidence-threshold gating is a cheap, reliable way to decide when to stop or escalate — because in this clinical setting, confidence and correctness are statistically decoupled.

A second, equally consequential failure mode is omission and "reasoning from silence." On a benchmark testing recognition of diagnostically informative *missing* information, frontier models largely fail to treat absence of evidence as absence of disease: reducing complete clinical histories to 20% of their content raised recognition of unanswerable/underspecified questions only from 5.2% to 13.8%, meaning models mostly guessed rather than flagging insufficient information ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)). The same paper found models identified relevant conditions in 94.9% of curated, clean vignettes, but this collapsed to **below 34.5%** when real patients conveyed problems through natural dialogue — a stark curated-benchmark-to-real-world gap. Across 4,249 real clinical management decisions, **76.6% of errors were omissions** (failing to recommend a needed action) rather than commissions (recommending an unnecessary one) — the opposite error distribution a pure efficiency-minded agent would expect to minimize ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)). The paper names four compounding **"behavioral inversions"**: credulity (models elaborate detailed reasoning around fabricated clinical details in 50–82% of cases), agreeableness (warmth-tuning increases error rates by causing models to affirm a user's incorrect belief), and miscalibration (choice-supportive bias and overconfidence under uncertainty) — with the net effect that "cases close before dangerous possibilities are excluded" ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)).

Multi-agent clinical systems add a fourth failure category that single-agent benchmarks cannot surface: false consensus. **MedAgentAudit**, auditing 3,600 cases across six medical datasets and six representative multi-agent frameworks, builds a taxonomy including "flawed consensus driven by shared model deficiencies," "suppression of correct minority opinions," "ineffective discussion dynamics" (debates devolving into opinion aggregation rather than evidence-based reasoning), "critical information loss during synthesis," and "superficial role-playing" (assigned expert personas failing to elicit genuinely different domain reasoning, producing homogeneous outputs) ([arXiv:2510.10185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.10185)). The notes flag that a widely circulated secondary claim — that over 68% of "successful" multi-agent cases reached the right answer through broken teamwork — comes from a social-media summary thread rather than confirmed primary-text extraction, and should not be cited as a verified MedAgentAudit statistic without independent verification. Separately, a GPT-4/Llama3 evaluation of FDA non-device clinical-decision-support compliance found that models readily produce "devicelike" (autonomous, directive) outputs even when explicitly instructed to stay within advisory bounds: for clinical emergency scenarios, **GPT-4 was 0% compliant** (recommending clinician-only interventions like IV antibiotic administration in sepsis) and Llama3 was 48% compliant, and under adversarial "jailbreak" prompting noncompliance rose to 80% (GPT-4) and 64% (Llama3) ([PMC11419185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11419185/)) — direct evidence that prompting alone cannot enforce a regulatory behavioral boundary.

These findings converge on the report's central structural claim. The clinical "asymmetric cost of error" is not a rhetorical framing but an explicit, quotable methodological argument in the primary literature: "the single catastrophic miss outweighs many false alarms," and this is "the logic of safe triage, which is asymmetric" and absent from both next-token pretraining and helpfulness-optimized alignment ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)). The paper's proposed behavioral metric explicitly weights "under-triage of must-not-miss conditions" as a penalized failure mode distinct from — and heavier than — general inaccuracy, and names the required safe behaviors explicitly: "widening under uncertainty, lowering the threshold for escalation, deferring when information is insufficient, resisting minimized...histories" — the literal opposite of efficiency-maximizing behaviors like early stopping, tight tool-call budgets, or rapid hypothesis-space narrowing ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)). The research notes are explicit that no source found connects this clinical asymmetric-cost argument to a specific general-domain efficiency paper and demonstrates the harm of porting it naively — this bridging argument does not yet exist in print and is a genuine contribution opportunity rather than an established finding.

One source requires an explicit flag before any further use: material retrieved under the label **arXiv:2607.05055**, searched as "Toward Trustworthy Large Language Model Agents in Healthcare," in fact returned content describing a system called **"CareConnect"** for clinical appointment scheduling. This title/content mismatch was not resolved during this research pass. The CareConnect-described mitigations (deterministic pre-LLM intent filtering that short-circuits emergency/diagnostic requests before the LLM is invoked, cached-state validation of tool invocations, structured JSON audit logging with correlation IDs) are plausible and specific enough to be useful design patterns, but **the paper's correct title, authorship, and arXiv identity must be independently re-verified — by fetching the arXiv abstract page directly rather than a rendered HTML mirror, or by a fresh targeted search — before this source is cited under either name in any actual paper draft.**

## Mitigations remove the model's free-text authority rather than trusting its self-reported confidence

A striking pattern recurs across every clinical mitigation located in this research: each one either (a) removes the LLM's free-text generation role for the specific high-stakes decision, or (b) adds a structurally independent verification step — rather than trusting the single agent's self-reported confidence. This is a strong signal, visible through what nobody proposes, that "ask the model how confident it is and gate on that" is viewed across this literature as insufficient, consistent with PMC12934906's direct finding that confidence does not predict correctness. On the ontology-constraint side, **SAFE-AI** combines expert clinical knowledge with an LLM in a rule-constrained architecture, reporting **97.9% accuracy** specifically on detecting epinephrine overdose errors, with hallucination "greatly mitigated when [the LLM is] limited to extracting information" under RAG rather than generating dosing text freely ([arXiv:2503.05777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05777)). **CLIN-LLM** layers "safety filters grounded in antibiotic stewardship rules and drug interaction checks" onto symptom classification and generation, with real-time uncertainty quantification flagging roughly **18% of ambiguous predictions for expert review** — explicitly justified even though the base system already reaches 81% accuracy on open-ended queries, because "even with 81% accuracy... human-in-the-loop mechanisms may still be warranted for real-world safety" ([arXiv:2510.22609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22609)).

On the verification side, the adversarial-guideline study's own authors — having shown confidence gating fails — propose (without yet validating) "independent verification and ranking safeguards before clinical deployment," "integrated multi-agent systems, orchestrator agents, judging LLMs," and chain-of-verification prompting, while explicitly caveating that their binary guideline-selection task "simplifies real-world agentic pipelines involving multiple tools, multi-document retrieval and reranking," and that future work should test whether these vulnerabilities **compound** across multi-tool pipelines ([PMC12934906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12934906/)). A harm-reduction viewpoint in JMIR frames LLMs as "assistive, never autonomous, tools" and recommends enforcing institutional human-in-the-loop validation policies for clinicians alongside patient-facing critical-health-literacy education ([JMIR 2025;27:e75849](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e75849)). Staged-autonomy proposals extend this into a graduated framework: rather than a binary human/AI split, clinical AI should progress through discrete autonomy levels tied to demonstrated, measured trustworthiness, explicitly balancing "excessive clinician burden" against "unsafe autonomous operation" ([arXiv:2604.26671](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.26671); this paper's PDF could not be parsed cleanly in this research pass, so mechanism specifics beyond this framing should be treated as secondary).

On the regulatory side, FDA's non-device clinical decision support (CDS) distinction — that compliant CDS may only "support" independent clinician judgment, never issue a "specific directive related to treatment or diagnosis" — functions as an external behavioral constraint that the empirical compliance-testing results above show prompting alone cannot satisfy, implying architectural (not merely prompt-level) enforcement is required ([PMC11419185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11419185/)). On the broader AI/ML Software-as-a-Medical-Device side, the FDA released draft guidance in January 2025 on Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC) management covering data-management practices, training-time overfitting controls, validation rigor, and documentation transparency; as of the search conducted for these notes, **no large language model is currently FDA-authorized as a clinical decision support device** (secondary-sourced via search synthesis — the original FDA.gov guidance document was not independently fetched in this research pass and should be pulled directly from fda.gov before being cited as a primary regulatory source). A Lancet Digital Health position piece independently argues that health-LLM applications face a structural regulatory mismatch — "high output variability, poor inherent explainability, and the risk of AI hallucinations" make standard device-approval pathways (US and EU, including the EU AI Act) hard to apply even while such applications are already on the market with "unaddressed risks for patients, including misdiagnosis and unverified medical advice" ([Lancet Digital Health, PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/article/PIIS2589-7500(24)00124-9/fulltext); this source returned an HTTP 403 on direct fetch in this research pass and its claims here rest on search-result summaries rather than verified full text). Taken together, no mitigation in this literature validates a *dynamic* policy that scales verification effort inversely with the model's own self-reported confidence — every proposed mechanism substitutes an externally-anchored signal (a rule, an ontology, a second independent agent, a human) precisely because self-reported confidence has been shown unreliable.

## Conclusion and gap analysis: where the general report's open problems become healthcare-specific research questions

Four convergent findings — the asymmetric must-not-miss cost argument ([arXiv:2607.28677](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28677)), general-domain evidence that aggressive early stopping is framed as "a good trade" (saving 28–64% of tokens for only 1.6–4.2 percentage points of accuracy loss in non-clinical settings; secondary-sourced and not independently verified against a single primary paper in this pass), general-domain evidence that agents "rarely turn their own evidence judgments into stopping decisions" without harness-level enforcement ([arXiv:2610.06191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06191)), and clinical evidence that 76.6% of real errors are omissions compounded by behavioral inversions that close cases "before dangerous possibilities are excluded" — sketch, without any single paper yet connecting them, exactly the argument this field is positioned to make: general-purpose tool-call-budget reduction is a documented good trade in low-stakes domains and a plausible catastrophe in domains where the failure distribution is omission-dominated and asymmetric. No paper in this research empirically tested that transfer. This is the clearest, most defensible gap-closing claim available, and it points directly to five concrete open directions that extend the general report's gap analysis into the medical domain.

First, and most directly answering the assignment's own framing: **no paper benchmarks a general-purpose efficiency technique (early-stopping, tool-call budgeting, confidence-threshold gating) against a clinical safety outcome rather than aggregate accuracy.** The ingredients exist separately — CROS's risk-constrained stopping formalism ([arXiv:2609.09678](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09678)), GraphDx's cost-utility test selection ([arXiv:2607.15280](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15280)), and the general-domain budget-aware-scaling literature cited in the companion report — but nobody has varied a tool-call budget or early-stopping aggressiveness on a shared clinical agent benchmark and reported the effect on under-triage/missed-must-not-miss rate specifically, rather than on headline accuracy. Second, **no controlled head-to-head exists between medical and general-domain tool-use accuracy under one shared evaluation protocol.** The BFCL-vs-FHIR-AgentBench/MedAgentBench juxtaposition in this report is a juxtaposition of differently-defined metrics on differently-constructed benchmarks, not a controlled comparison — and the discovery that BFCL v4 itself carries a 20% task-construction error rate ([arXiv:2607.02577](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02577)) means even the reference point this comparison would need is independently unreliable. Third, **the asymmetric-cost argument implies "optimal" tool-use in healthcare may need a fundamentally different objective function than cost/latency minimization** — not merely a reweighted one. arXiv:2607.28677's call for "widening under uncertainty" and "lowering the threshold for escalation" is directionally opposite to every general-domain efficiency metric surveyed (tokens saved, calls avoided, latency reduced); no paper has proposed, let alone validated, a formal decision-theoretic objective that trades resource cost against false-negative cost in a clinical tool-use setting with a calibrated, externally-anchored (not self-reported) utility weighting — CROS is the closest extant attempt but optimizes an error-rate *constraint*, not a jointly-weighted asymmetric-loss objective. Fourth, **no paper has tested whether the adversarial-guideline-trust vulnerability and the multi-agent false-consensus vulnerability compound when combined in one realistic multi-tool pipeline** — PMC12934906 explicitly names this as its own limitation, and MedAgentAudit's taxonomy was built on existing multi-agent frameworks rather than under adversarial-tool conditions; a workshop paper could be the first to run a poisoned-guideline-retrieval tool inside a multi-agent clinical team and measure whether false consensus amplifies or dampens the underlying 59.4%-accuracy vulnerability. Fifth, and more exploratory: **the two clinical benchmark families (dialogue-mediated simulated tools vs. schema-constrained FHIR/API tools) have never been unified or cross-validated against each other** — a model's AgentClinic-style diagnostic-dialogue competence and its FHIR-AgentBench-style structured-tool-calling competence are currently measured as if they were unrelated skills, despite both being framed as "clinical tool use"; whether they correlate, and whether a single architecture (e.g., MDAgents' complexity-adaptive team recruitment) transfers gains across both, is untested.

The deepest implication of this research is that "tool-use optimization," as a research program, may not transfer across domains by simply re-deriving the same efficiency techniques under a stricter accuracy floor. The general report's framing — a cheap first pass gated by a stricter validator, repeated across retrieval, caching, and calibration — survives in medicine only when the validator is anchored to something external to the model's own outputs (an ontology, a deterministic rule, a human, a second independently-reasoning agent), because the clinical literature has specifically falsified the general-domain assumption that a model's own confidence is a usable gating signal. A workshop paper building on this ground should therefore frame healthcare not as a harder instance of the same optimization problem, but as a domain where the sign of several terms in the objective function — call cost, stopping threshold, verification overhead — plausibly flips, and where demonstrating that flip empirically, on a clinical safety outcome rather than an aggregate accuracy number, is the open contribution nobody has yet made.
